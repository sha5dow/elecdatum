## Overview
Powder coating is a dry coating process in which a mixture of thermoset or thermoplastic polymers in the form of fine powder is electrostatically applied onto a surface, typically metallic, and cured by heat or ultraviolet radiation to form a continuous, durable, high-thickness film. Unlike conventional liquid coatings, it uses no solvents, resulting in virtually zero volatile organic compound (VOC) emissions and allowing recovery of overspray powder, thereby reducing waste.

Typical particle size ranges from 2 to 50 µm (0.08 to 2.0 mils), with a melting temperature around 150 °C (302 °F) and standard curing at 200 °C (392 °F) for 10 to 15 minutes. With special formulations, films from 30 µm (1.2 mils) to over 150 µm (5.9 mils) can be achieved, with 60–80 µm (2.4–3.1 mils) being the most common range for industrial use.

## Operating principle
Application relies on electrostatic attraction between the part to be coated and the powder particles. The part, previously cleaned and often treated with a conversion coating (phosphating or chromating) to improve adhesion, acts as ground or is negatively charged. The powder is fluidized in a hopper and conveyed by compressed air to the spray gun, where it receives an electrostatic charge (typically negative by corona effect or tribocharging). The charged particles are projected toward the part and adhere uniformly due to electrostatic forces, even partially wrapping around rear faces because of air currents. Once the desired layer is deposited, the part is placed in a curing oven, where the powder melts, flows, and chemically crosslinks (for thermosets) or simply melts and solidifies (thermoplastics), forming a continuous film.

## Applicable materials
Powder coating is primarily used on metals such as steel, stainless steel, aluminum, galvanized parts, and castings. With UV-cure or low-heat technology, temperature-sensitive substrates can be coated: composites, carbon fiber, plastics, MDF, and even solid wood. Selection of powder type (epoxy, polyester, acrylic, polyurethane, epoxy-polyester hybrid) depends on final requirements for chemical resistance, weatherability, or flexibility. Pretreatments such as phosphating (for steel) or chromating (for aluminum and zinc) improve adhesion and anticorrosion protection; typical thicknesses of these conversion layers are 1–25 µm (0.04–1.0 mils) for anodizing and approximately 0.025 µm (1 µin) for chromating.

| Substrate material | Recommended pretreatment | Most common powder type | Remarks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Carbon steel | Phosphating (iron/zinc) | Epoxy, hybrid | Good anticorrosion protection |
| Stainless steel | Blasting + passivation | Polyester, acrylic | Requires good surface preparation |
| Aluminum | Chromating, anodizing | Polyester TGIC, polyurethane | Frequent outdoor use |
| Cast iron | Phosphating, primer | Epoxy | Metal degassing necessary |
| Galvanized steel | Phosphating, wash primer | Polyester, hybrid | Critical adhesion in humid environments |
| Plastics/composites | Cleaning, adhesion promoter | Thermoplastic (polyethylene, polyamide), UV | Low-temperature curing |
| MDF/Wood | Sanding, preheating | Low-Tg thermoset | Strict humidity control required |

## Application parameters
The following table lists typical operating parameters for the dry electrostatic process and oven curing. Values may vary depending on equipment, powder type, and part geometry.

| Parameter | Typical value | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| Charge voltage (Corona) | 50 – 100 kV | Voltage (kilovolts) |
| Spray current | 10 – 50 µA | Microamperes |
| Air flow rate | 3 – 7 m³/h / 1.8 – 4.1 cfm | Volumetric flow |
| Gun-to-part distance | 150 – 300 mm / 5.9 – 11.8 in | Distance |
| Powder melting temperature | ~150 °C / 302 °F | Temperature |
| Curing temperature | 180 – 220 °C / 356 – 428 °F (typical 200 °C / 392 °F) | Temperature |
| Curing time (from when part reaches temperature) | 10 – 20 min, typical 15 min | Time |
| Applicable film thickness | 30 – 150 µm / 1.2 – 5.9 mils (standard 60–80 µm / 2.4–3.1 mils) | Micrometers / mils |
| Powder particle size | 2 – 50 µm / 0.08 – 2.0 mils (optimized 30–40 µm / 1.2–1.6 mils) | Micrometers / mils |

For UV-cure powders, parameters are adjusted: melting is typically done with infrared heat and then crosslinked with UV radiation in seconds, allowing part temperatures below 100 °C (212 °F) and suitable for heat-sensitive materials.

## Advantages
- **Solvent-free**: the process emits no VOCs, simplifying environmental compliance and workplace safety.
- **High thickness in one coat**: films up to 150 µm (5.9 mils) can be achieved without runs, a thickness that would require multiple coats in liquid paint.
- **Excellent durability and mechanical resistance**: thermoset cures provide high hardness, impact resistance, and chemical resistance, superior to many baking enamels.
- **Overspray powder recovery**: overspray can be collected and reused, achieving transfer efficiencies over 95% with recycling systems.
- **Capability for special finishes**: allows textures, metallic effects, wrinkle or hammer finishes, as well as applying multiple colors before curing for blending or soft edges.
- **Prolonged anticorrosion protection**: combined with appropriate pretreatments, it withstands hundreds of hours in salt spray, making it ideal for outdoors and aggressive environments.

## Limitations
- **Difficulty obtaining thin, smooth coats**: below 50 µm (2.0 mils), orange peel tends to appear due to particle size and powder Tg. Ultrathin coats (<30 µm) require special formulations or the powder slurry in water process.
- **High initial investment**: a complete system (booth, guns, curing oven, recovery system) has a cost comparable to professional liquid paint equipment, but operational complexity can be higher.
- **Color limitations in multi-use processes**: if multiple colors are used in the same booth, powder recycling becomes complicated, reducing efficiency and potentially causing color contamination.
- **High curing temperatures**: most conventional powders require at least 180 °C (356 °F) metal temperature, preventing direct use on plastics or pre-assembled components with heat-sensitive elements.
- **Critical surface preparation**: adhesion and corrosion resistance depend heavily on thorough cleaning and application of a conversion coating (phosphating or chromating), adding steps and costs.

## Selection guide
To choose between powder coating and other industrial coating alternatives, consider the following criteria:

- **Thickness and durability requirements**: if a protective coat of 60–150 µm with high impact and weather resistance is needed, powder coating is the most robust option. For coats below 30 µm with a specular finish, liquid paint or electrodeposition coating may offer better surface smoothness.
- **Part geometry**: electrostatic wrapping facilitates coating of complex shapes, but deep cavities or hidden faces may require guns with specific electrodes.
- **High-volume production and color variety**: on high-production lines with one or few fixed colors, recovery and automation make powder a very efficient solution. If frequent color changes are required, quick-change booths or isolated cartridge systems will be needed.
- **Substrate type and process temperature**: for metals that withstand 180–200 °C (356–392 °F), powder coating is ideal; for plastics or composites, UV-cure powders or low-temperature formulations (120–150 °C / 248–302 °F) must be chosen.
- **Regulatory and environmental requirements**: in facilities with strict VOC restrictions, powder coating eliminates solvent use and simplifies emission management.

## Frequently asked questions

1. **What is the recommended thickness for a first coat of powder coating?** The standard dry film thickness ranges from 60 to 80 µm (2.4–3.1 mils), although on parts exposed to severe corrosion, up to 120–150 µm (4.7–5.9 mils) can be applied.

2. **At what temperature is a thermoset powder coating normally cured?** The oven temperature is maintained around 200 °C (392 °F), and the part must remain at that temperature for at least 10–15 minutes for chemical crosslinking to complete.

3. **What particle size does the powder have for smooth finishes?** For a finish without significant orange peel, fractions with a mean diameter of 30–40 µm (1.2–1.6 mils) are used, although the overall product distribution is typically between 2 and 50 µm (0.08–2.0 mils).

4. **How much overspray powder can be recovered in a system with recycling?** In automated booths with closed-loop recycling, transfer efficiency can exceed 95%, recovering virtually all powder that does not deposit on the part.

5. **What salt spray resistance does a powder coating offer on phosphated steel?** A polyester coating of 80 µm (3.1 mils) on steel with zinc phosphating typically withstands over 500 hours in salt spray testing according to ISO 9227 without blistering or corrosion at the scribe.

6. **Can powder coating be applied to plastic?** Yes, using UV-cure powders that melt at temperatures below 100 °C (212 °F) and harden in seconds under ultraviolet radiation, allowing treatment of plastics, wood, and composites without deforming the substrate.
