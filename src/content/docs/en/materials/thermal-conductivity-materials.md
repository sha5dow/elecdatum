## Chemical composition
Aluminum alloys for thermal applications contain between 3% and 5% copper, such as duralumin (94‑96% Al, 3‑5% Cu) and silumin (87% Al, 13% Si). Brasses present 70‑85% copper with additions of zinc (30‑15%) and tin up to 9%. Type 304 stainless steel has an iron base composition with 18‑20% chromium and 8‑10.5% nickel. At the opposite end, unfilled polymers are composed of long organic chains of monomers such as ethylene, propylene or tetrafluoroethylene, whose intrinsic macromolecular structure limits phonon transfer.

## Mechanical properties
Metals with thermal conductivity above 200 W/(m·K), such as electrolytic copper, maintain a tensile strength in annealed condition of at least 210 MPa, while alloyed aluminum 2014 reaches 190 W/(m·K) with yield strength above 240 MPa in T6 treatment. The addition of conductive particles in polymer composites allows raising the Young modulus from less than 2 GPa for pure polyethylene to nearly 5 GPa with 30% by volume fillers, at the cost of a reduction in elongation at break.

## Physical and thermal properties
Thermal conductivity values range from 0.1‑0.5 W/(m·K) for unfilled polymers to 428 W/(m·K) for pure silver at 0 °C. Below are the conductivities of metals and engineering alloys as a function of temperature, as well as the behavior of polymer composites with high-conductivity particles.

| Material | Temperature (°C) | Thermal conductivity k (W/m·K) |
|---|---|---|
| Silver (pure) | 0 | 428 |
| Copper (pure) | 0 | 401 |
| Aluminum (pure) | 0 | 236 |
| Duralumin (94‑96% Al) | 20 | 164 |
| Cartridge brass (UNS C26000) | 20 | 120 |
| Aluminum 3003, rolled | 0‑25 | 190 |
| Phosphor bronze (10% Sn) | 20 | 50 |
| Constantan (60% Cu, 40% Ni) | 20 | 22.7 |
| 304 Stainless steel | 20 | 14.4 |
| Cast iron | 20 | 52 |
| Nickel (pure) | 0 | 94 |
| Titanium (pure) | 0 | 22.4 |
| Tungsten (pure) | 0 | 182 |
| Polymer composite with HC particles* | 20 | 1‑20 |

*Significant increase compared to the base polymer (<0.5 W/m·K) due to addition of ceramic, metallic or carbon fillers; the final value depends on the volumetric content, particle shape and quality of the matrix‑filler interface.

## Applications by industry
Thermal conductivity determines the use of each material in sectors where 150‑400 W/(m·K) are required for dissipation and 0.1‑15 W/(m·K) for insulation. In the electronics industry, copper and aluminum are used for heat sinks and encapsulations; the aerospace sector uses high-conductivity polymer composites for lightweight housings that evacuate heat; automotive applies aluminum 3003 in radiators and filled polymers in battery casings; construction uses stainless steels and insulating polymers in thermal windows; and the energy sector employs nickel alloys and constantan in heat exchangers and thermocouples.

## Comparison with similar materials
A material with 400 W/(m·K) transfers approximately 20 times more heat than 304 stainless steel and up to 800 times more than a virgin polymer. Compared to metals, polymer composites filled with particles offer 4‑5 times lower density and superior chemical resistance, although their maximum conductivity in commercial applications rarely exceeds 20 W/(m·K). Copper and aluminum alloys dominate when conductivity >100 W/(m·K) combined with moderate mechanical strength is required, while alloyed steels are reserved for corrosive or high-temperature environments where conductivity is not a priority.

| Property | Metals (Cu, Al) | HC polymer composites | Advantage |
|---|---|---|---|
| Typical conductivity (W/m·K) | 120‑401 | 1‑20 | Metals |
| Density (g/cm³) | 2.7‑8.9 | 1.0‑1.8 | Polymers |
| Chemical resistance | Medium | High | Polymers |
| Relative processing cost | Medium | Low | Polymers |

## Frequently asked questions
*   Which metal has the highest thermal conductivity? Silver has the highest conductivity among metals, with 428 W/(m·K) at 0 °C, although copper is the most used industrially with 401 W/(m·K) at 0 °C due to its lower cost.
*   How does temperature affect the conductivity of aluminum? Pure aluminum maintains 237 W/(m·K) at -73 °C and 220 W/(m·K) at 527 °C; the decrease is gradual up to 327 °C and accelerates slightly above this temperature.
*   What conductivity value does 304 stainless steel have? 304 stainless steel has a conductivity of 14.4 W/(m·K) at 20 °C, almost 30 times lower than pure copper.
*   Can a polymer reach metallic conductivities? Polymer composites with high-conductivity particles can reach up to 20 W/(m·K), a value comparable to stainless steel, but still far below copper or aluminum.
*   What is the typical conductivity of phosphor bronze? Bronze with 10% tin (UNS C52400) has 50 W/(m·K) at 20 °C, placing it in an intermediate range between steels and brasses.
*   What material is recommended for a lightweight heat exchanger? Aluminum alloys such as 3003 rolled offer 190 W/(m·K) with a density of only 2.7 g/cm³, being preferable to steels or copper when weight is critical.
