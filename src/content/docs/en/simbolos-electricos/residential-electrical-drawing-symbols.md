Residential electrical drawings use more than 40 standardized symbols to unambiguously represent the components of an installation. Their correct interpretation avoids assembly errors and reduces execution time on site.

## Symbol table for residential electrical installations

The following table compiles the 32 most common symbols in housing plans, with their graphical representation, designation, and function.

| Symbol | Designation | Function |

| Circle with two vertical parallel lines, a horizontal line, and a second oblique line joining the ends | Three-way switch (commutation) | Allows turning a load on or off from two different points (staircase). |
| Circle with two vertical parallel lines and a horizontal line with a small “S” superimposed | Dimmer switch | Varies the luminous intensity of a compatible lamp. |
| Open semicircle facing upward with a vertical line and a small arc in the center | Push button (doorbell) | Activates a circuit only while pressed (doorbell, call). |
| Circle with a horizontal line and two external arcs in an open “V” shape on the sides | Double-pole switch | Simultaneously cuts phase and neutral; used for air conditioning or water heaters. |
| Rectangle with a vertical line and a pair of slanted lines on the upper side | Pull chain switch | Actuation by chain, typical in bathrooms or closets. |
| Filled circle with a line crossing through and a small perpendicular line at the end | Double outlet without grounding | Two-pin socket, for general use without ground contact. |
| Filled circle with a line and three small parallel lines at the bottom | Double outlet with grounding (Schuko or similar) | Socket with protective earth contact (universal outlet in housing). |

## International standards and equivalents

More than 1 900 standardized symbols are defined in the **IEC 60617** series (Graphical representation for diagrams) and in the **ANSI/IEEE 315** standard (Graphic Symbols for Electrical and Electronics Diagrams). In the residential field, **IEC 60617-11** (installation plans) and **ISO 14617** are mainly used; in North America, the **NFPA 170** standard is also commonly applied.

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

The following are 6 common questions about the symbology used in residential electrical plans.

### What is the difference between a single-line and multi-line symbol in residential plans?

The single-line diagram represents all conductors of a circuit with a single line; it is used in residential distribution plans to simplify the layout. The multi-line diagram draws each conductor separately and is more common in control diagrams or where internal connections need to be detailed.

### Which standard governs electrical symbols in residential plans?

In most countries, the international standard **IEC 60617-11** is adopted, which contains representations for architectural and installation diagrams. National adaptations may exist, such as **IRAM 2260** in Argentina or **NCh 4/2003** in Chile, always based on IEC.

### How is a three-way switch (staircase) represented in a plan?

A circle with two terminals, one supply line, and two outputs to the other switch is drawn. The complete symbol consists of two switches joined by two parallel oblique lines indicating the link conductors; the light point is connected between both units.

### What is the standardized symbol for a grounded outlet?

According to IEC 60617, it is represented with a semicircle (or filled circle) and a horizontal line ending in three small parallel lines, one of them longer, indicating the ground contact. In practice, it is accompanied by the initials “2P+T” or the angular ground symbol.

### Are there regional variations in residential symbology?

Yes. Although most standards align with IEC, in North America a representation based on ANSI/IEEE is preferred, and some European countries maintain their own variants, especially in the way of indicating staircase switches and outlets. In an international project, it is advisable to always attach a symbol legend.

### What complementary information accompanies the symbols in an electrical plan?

Each symbol usually has an alphanumeric indication next to it that identifies the circuit (for example, I-1 for lighting, TUG-2 for general purpose outlets) and, in the case of luminaires, the nominal power. Additionally, location dimensions, mounting height, and references to the conduit plan are included.
